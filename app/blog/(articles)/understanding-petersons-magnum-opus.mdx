If I told you circles weren't real, then you'd probably want to tell me I'm an idiot.<br />
But, if I tell you god isn't real, then I'm entitled to my own opinion...

_I'm not going to comment on any political commentary here, and I'm going to focus on his psychological and philsophical works._

Humans learn primarily through two methods: mistakes and imitation.

First I want to focus on the aspect of learning from mistakes.<br />
Evolutionarily, this presents quite a problem. Mainly that our brains are limited in capacity, but our world is vast and complex.

Lets think for a second that I touch a hot pan sitting on a stove. My brain will register the pain and I will pull my hand away.<br />
I will learn quickly, that I do not like the pain and will avoid the pan in the future.<br />
But this presents a problem, what was it exactly that I didn't like? Was it that specific pan, or the colour, or the texture?<br />

We can't learn from every single interaction, so we need to find patterns.<br />
Our brains do something genius that allows us to learn ideas, devoid of their particular context.<br />
We don't have to test every single pan, and remember every single pan we've touched.<br />
We don't need to remember every single pan, every detail, what a pan is, which pans are more dangerous than others.<br />
Instead what we do is we abstract the pan into the cateogories of "hot" and "dangerous".<br />

So lets say I take a piece of paper, and I draw a circle on it. Then I ask you to go walk the earth and find me a circle.<br />
You can walk the whole earth, but you will never find a circle, unless another human has created one previously.<br />
You may find objects that look like a circle, such as a clock, or a coin, but you will never find a perfect circle devoid of its context.<br />

The circle is an example of the brain abstracting patterns from the world, and creating a general idea of what category objects belong to, despite the fact there's no evidence of that category existing outside of conscious interpretation.<br />

Despite the fact that the circle is an anomily, no one seems to notice, nor do they care.<br />
People will suspend their disbelief and accept the circle as a real object, since it's useful to do so.<br />

This is a key aspect of human psychology. Humans don't care about what is and isn't real in a literal sense, they care about what is meaningful and what is useful.<br />

Peterson seperates the world into two domains, the domain of the literal, and the domain of the meaningful.<br />

The domain of the literal is the realm of science, where we can test and verify ideas.<br />
We ask "what is that, and how is it useful to me?"<br />

The domain of the meaningful is the realm of art, where we can create and interpret ideas.<br />
We ask "what does this mean, and how is it useful to me?"<br />

The domain of the literal is objective, while the domain of the meaningful is subjective.<br />

But the domain of the literal depends on the subjective, as we need to have an emotional goal to determine what is useful to us in the first place.<br />

Another way we learn is by imitation. We pick a goal, and then find someone who is good at that goal, and then we imitate their actions in an attempt to recreate their successes.<br />

So Peterson poses the question, "What is the goal?".<br />
The goal is the emotional state we want to achieve, and the actions we take are the means to achieve that goal.<br />
Historically, throughough the tradition of western philsophy, the goal has been broadly utilitarian.<br />
The goal is to be good, and to be good is to be someone whose actions maximise the well-being of others.<br />

Just like our circle, or our hot pan, we don't need to examine someones whole life to determine if they are good or bad.<br />
Instead we can do something much simpler and less cognitively taxing.<br />
We examine many peoples actions, then abstract it to a category of what we call "good" or "bad".<br />
We can then imitate the actions of the good people, and avoid the actions of the bad people.<br />

This is a really great strategy, because it avoids the possibility of, to put it blunty, death.<br />
If we had to try every single action and examine every single person in detail we'd never have enough time to do so.<br />

So who is the most good?<br />

This question draws upon one of Petersons rules for life.<br />
"Don't compare yourself to others, compare yourself only to who you were yesterday".<br />
If you're good at one thing, you may be bad at another. The person who is the best in the world at basketball may be bad at mathematics.<br />

Lets say you want to live your life in a way that helps other people want to give you the maximum amount of opportunities to pursue your goals.<br />

Who then, would people admire the most?<br />

One possible way to answer this question is to look at the category of successful people who are admired and then the category of their actions which people admire.<br />

Maybe someone you want to help, is someone who is willing to die to redeem the world (yourself included).<br />

What if those set of behaviours which you admire the most in a person were explainable in a story.<br />
What if the story was shared by many people, and the story was the same for all of them.<br />
It would be like the instruction manual, or operating system of human existence.<br />
_The philosophical framework for humans learned from imitation and mistakes._

If those set of admirable behaviours all revolved around a central theme, then that theme would be the most important aspect of human existence.<br />
For without a set of admirable behaviours, there would be no reason to imitate them.<br />
Without admirable behaviours, there's no goal.<br />
With no goal, there's no meaning.<br />

If you were confronted with a set of behaviours which were maximially admirable, but not found in the immediate environment around you, would you believe in them?<br />
Could you suspend disbelief and accept the idea that those behaviours are real, and that they are useful to you?<br />
Just like you believe in a circle?<br />
